Welcome! If this is your first time using a computer, you have come to the right place. We will take it slow, and you will be typing on your own within a few minutes.

What is a keyboard?

The keyboard is the flat rectangle with many small buttons (called keys) in front of you. Each key does something different. Pressing the letter keys types that letter.

Special keys you will use every day

  • Space bar — the long key at the bottom. It adds a blank space between words.
  • Enter (or Return) — the big key on the right. It starts a new line or confirms a choice.
  • Shift — hold it down while pressing a letter to make a capital letter.
  • Backspace — deletes the character to the left of your cursor. Use it to fix mistakes.
  • Caps Lock — makes every letter capital until you press it again. Watch out for this one!

The home row

Place your fingers on these keys, called the home row:

A S D F   J K L ;

Your left hand sits on A S D F and your right hand sits on J K L ;. Your two thumbs rest on the space bar.

The F and J keys usually have small raised bumps. You can find them by feel without looking. That is your anchor point.

Where do the other letters live?

Every letter key is one row above or below the home row. You reach for them with the nearest finger, then return to the home row.

Do not worry about speed yet. Speed comes from accuracy. Type slowly and correctly, and the speed will follow.

Tip: Do not look at the keyboard while you type. Look at the screen instead. It feels strange at first, but after a few days your fingers will know where the keys are.

You can do most things on a computer much faster using keyboard shortcuts. A shortcut is a combination of keys you press at the same time. They save you from clicking through menus.

The most useful shortcuts

  • Ctrl + C — Copy. Remembers whatever you have selected.
  • Ctrl + V — Paste. Puts the copied item where the cursor is.
  • Ctrl + X — Cut. Like Copy, but removes the original.
  • Ctrl + Z — Undo. Reverses your last action. Use this when you make a mistake!
  • Ctrl + S — Save. Saves the file you are working on.
  • Ctrl + A — Select All. Highlights everything in the current window.
  • Ctrl + F — Find. Searches for a word on the page or document.

On a Mac, use Cmd instead of Ctrl.

How to use them

Hold the Ctrl key down, then press the other key, then release both. For example:

  1. Hold Ctrl down with your little finger (left hand).
  2. Press C with your index finger.
  3. Release both. The item is now copied.

Why this matters

If you write emails, fill in forms, or apply for jobs online, you will use copy and paste constantly. Copy your ID number once, paste it wherever it is needed. Copy a job description, paste it into a document to prepare for the interview.

Ctrl + Z is your safety net. If you ever delete something by accident, press Ctrl + Z immediately. It will come back.

Practice tip: Every time you use a mouse or trackpad to do something, ask yourself: is there a shortcut for this? After a week, you will be twice as fast.

Everything on a computer is a file — a document, a photo, a song, a video. Files are stored inside folders, which are like the paper folders in a filing cabinet.

What is a file name?

Every file has a name, and the name ends with a dot and a few letters. That ending is called the extension. It tells the computer what kind of file it is.

The extensions you will see most

  • .docx — Word document (a letter, CV, report)
  • .pdf — A document that looks the same on every computer (job adverts, contracts, certificates)
  • .jpg or .png — A picture or photo
  • .mp3 — A song or audio recording
  • .mp4 — A video
  • .xlsx — Excel spreadsheet (tables of numbers)
  • .zip — A bundle of files packed together (smaller to download)

Why extensions matter

The extension tells the computer which program to open the file with. A .docx file opens in Word. A .jpg opens in your image viewer. A .pdf opens in a PDF reader.

If you rename a .jpg file to .docx, the computer will try to open it in Word — and it will fail. The extension is not just a label. It is an instruction.

What about folders?

Folders hold files. You can also put folders inside folders. This lets you organise things clearly.

A typical arrangement:

My Documents/
├── Job Applications/
│   ├── CV.docx
│   └── Cover Letter.docx
├── Personal/
│   └── National ID.pdf
└── Photos/
    └── Graduation.jpg

When you need your CV, you know exactly where to look. No more searching through a messy desktop.

Good habit: the moment you save a new file, put it in the right folder and give it a clear name. "CV 2025 - Chisomo Banda.docx" is much better than "document1.docx".

Now that you know what files and folders are, let's practise organising them. This is the skill that will save you the most time over the years.

Step 1: Create a folder before you save

Before starting anything new, create a folder for it. Right-click on the desktop or inside another folder, then choose New > Folder. Type the folder name and press Enter.

Good folder names are short and clear: Job Applications, not Stuff or New Folder (3).

Step 2: Save into the right folder

When you write a document, use Save As (or press Ctrl + Shift + S in most programs). A window opens showing your folders. Click into the right one, give the file a good name, and save.

Step 3: Rename files when they are unclear

Right-click on a file and choose Rename. Change it to something you will recognise in six months. "Invoice - Airtel - March 2025.pdf" is far better than "doc.pdf".

Step 4: Move files into folders

Click a file once to select it, then drag it into a folder. On a phone or tablet, press and hold the file, then choose Move and pick the folder.

Step 5: Delete what you do not need

Right-click and choose Delete. On most systems, the file goes to a Recycle Bin (Windows) or Trash (Mac). It stays there until you empty it — so if you delete something by mistake, you can still get it back.

Naming rules that will save you

  • Use spaces or underscores: Cover Letter.docx or Cover_Letter.docx
  • Include the date: 2025-03-15 Invoice.pdf
  • Use a consistent style. If you use dates, always use the same date format.
  • Avoid symbols like / \ : * ? " < > | — the computer does not allow them in file names.
Tip: A well-named file is a gift to your future self. When you are in a hurry and someone asks for your CV, you want to find it in three seconds — not thirty.

Email is still the most important tool on the internet for work and job applications. In Malawi, most employers ask for applications by email. Learning to write a clear email is a life skill.

Setting up an email account

You can create a free email account in about five minutes. The most common options are:

  • Gmail (Google) — gmail.com. The most widely used in Malawi.
  • Outlook (Microsoft) — outlook.com. Popular in offices.
  • Yahoo Mail — mail.yahoo.com. Older but still works.

You will need a phone number to verify your account, and a password that you will remember. Use a strong password — we cover that in Module 4.

Choosing your email address

Your email address says something about you. Choose something professional:

  • Good: chisomo.banda@gmail.com, c.banda.mw@gmail.com
  • Avoid: sexyboy2003@gmail.com, chisomo_loves_music@yahoo.com

If you already have an informal address, create a new one for job applications. It is free.

What does an email look like?

Every email has five parts:

  1. To — the email address you are sending to. For a job, this is the HR department: hr@company.mw.
  2. Subject — one short line that says what the email is about. "Application for Accounts Assistant position" is perfect.
  3. Body — the message itself. Keep it short and clear.
  4. Attachment — any files you are sending (your CV, a certificate).
  5. Signature — your name and phone number at the bottom, so they can contact you.

The perfect job application email

To: hr@techmountain.mw
Subject: Application for Junior Developer Position

Dear Sir/Madam,

I am writing to apply for the Junior Developer position
advertised on your website on 10 March 2025.

I have completed the HTML, CSS and JavaScript courses at
TechMountain Bootcamp and I am confident that I can
contribute to your team. My CV and certificates are attached.

I am available for an interview at any time.

Thank you for your consideration.

Yours faithfully,
Chisomo Banda
+265 999 555 123
chisomo.banda@gmail.com

Notice how short it is. Five short paragraphs. That is all you need.

Rule: Never leave the Subject blank. An email without a subject looks careless. It may be deleted without being read.

A well-written email gets a reply. A carelessly written one does not. Let's cover the small things that make a big difference.

Replying vs replying to all

  • Reply — sends only to the person who wrote to you.
  • Reply All — sends to everyone who was on the original email. Use this only when everyone needs to know.

If a job advert was sent to 20 people and you click Reply All, all 20 see your reply. That is embarrassing. When in doubt, use Reply.

The greeting

  • If you know the person's name: Dear Mr Banda, or Dear Mrs Phiri,
  • If you do not know the name: Dear Sir/Madam, or Dear Hiring Manager,
  • To a friend: Hi Chisomo, or Hello,

The sign-off

  • Yours faithfully, — when the greeting was Dear Sir/Madam
  • Yours sincerely, — when you used the person's name
  • Best regards, or Kind regards, — safe, professional, works everywhere
  • Thanks, — fine for a colleague or friend, less formal for a first email

Attachments

Before you click Send, always ask: did I actually attach the file? The most common email mistake in the world is writing "please find attached" and forgetting to attach.

Tips for attachments:

  • Attach your CV as a PDF, not Word. PDFs look the same everywhere.
  • Name the file properly: CV - Chisomo Banda.pdf, not cv.pdf.
  • Keep total attachments under 10 MB. Big files may not send.
  • If you must send many files, zip them into one file.

Email etiquette

  • Reply within 24 hours if possible. Even a short "thank you, I will reply tomorrow" is better than silence.
  • Do not write in all caps. CAPS LOCK LOOKS LIKE YOU ARE SHOUTING.
  • Do not use too many exclamation marks!!! One is enough.
  • Proofread before sending. Read it out loud. Fix typos. Check names.
  • Do not forward chain letters or scam messages. They waste everyone's time.
The rule of email: write it as if the whole world will read it. Because in a way, it will — emails can be forwarded, printed, and screenshotted. Never write anything you would be embarrassed to see on a screen in front of your boss.

The internet is wonderful. It also contains people who want to trick you. Learning to spot them is not paranoia — it is the digital equivalent of locking your front door.

Passwords: your first line of defence

A weak password takes a criminal about one second to guess. A strong one takes years.

Weak passwords (never use these):

  • password, 123456, qwerty
  • Your name, phone number, or birthday
  • Your child's name or your village

Strong passwords:

  • At least 12 characters long
  • Mix of upper and lower case, numbers, and symbols
  • Not a word in the dictionary
  • Different for every account

A good pattern: pick three random words and join them with a symbol and a number. Mango!Chair7River is long and easy to remember, but hard to guess.

Never use the same password twice. If one site is hacked, criminals will try the same password on your email, your bank, and your Facebook.

What is phishing?

Phishing is when a criminal sends you a message pretending to be from a company you trust. They want you to click a link and enter your password, or send them money.

Common phishing messages look like:

  • "Your Airtel Money account has been suspended. Click here to reactivate."
  • "Congratulations! You have won MWK 500,000. Send your details to claim."
  • "Your parcel is waiting. Pay a small fee to release it."

How to spot a phishing message

  • Urgency. "Act now!" "Your account will be closed in 24 hours!" Real companies do not threaten you like this.
  • Wrong email address. The sender may look like support@airtel-mw-secure.com instead of support@airtel.mw. Read carefully.
  • Bad spelling or grammar. Real companies proofread.
  • They ask for your password or PIN. No legitimate company will ever ask for your password, PIN, or full card number.
  • Links that do not match. Hover over a link before clicking. The real destination might be different from what is shown.

What to do if you receive one

  1. Do not click. Do not reply. Do not call any number in the message.
  2. Delete the message, or report it as spam.
  3. If it claims to be from your bank or Airtel, call them directly on the official number from their website to check.
The golden rule: If a message asks for your password, PIN, or money, it is almost certainly a scam. Real banks never ask. Real Airtel never asks. Real Microsoft never calls you out of the blue.

The internet is safe when you use it carefully. This lesson covers the everyday habits that keep you, your money, and your family safe online.

At internet cafés

Internet cafés are useful, but shared computers carry risks.

  • Always log out of email and social media when you finish. Not just close the window — actually click Log Out.
  • Never check your bank or mobile money at a café if you can avoid it.
  • Never let the computer remember your password. If it asks, say no.
  • Clear the browser history when you finish, especially on a shared computer.
  • Do not plug in a USB drive you do not trust. It might contain a virus.

On public WiFi

  • Free WiFi at a mall or hotel is usually safe for browsing but risky for banking. Save banking for your home connection.
  • If you must use public WiFi for something sensitive, look for the padlock icon in your browser. It means the connection is encrypted.
  • Turn off file sharing when you are on public WiFi.

Downloading files and apps

  • Only download from official websites — the real microsoft.com, the real whatsapp.com, or your phone's official app store.
  • Never download "free" versions of paid software from unknown sites. They often come with viruses.
  • Never open an attachment from someone you do not know, even if the subject looks interesting.

What you share online

Everything you post online can live forever. Before you post a photo, ask yourself: would I be happy if my employer saw this in 5 years?

Never share publicly:

  • Your ID number or passport number
  • Your home address
  • Your bank or mobile money PIN
  • Photos showing your bank card or phone number
  • Where your children go to school

Social media safety

  • Set your profile to private. Not everyone needs to see your posts.
  • Do not accept friend requests from strangers, especially if they look like fake profiles.
  • If someone asks for money, gifts, or your bank details — stop the conversation. It is a scam.
  • Never send money to someone you met only online, no matter what story they tell.

If something goes wrong

  1. Change your password immediately on the affected account and any account that used the same password.
  2. Enable two-factor authentication if the service offers it. You will get a code on your phone each time you log in.
  3. Tell your bank or mobile money provider straight away if money is involved.
  4. Tell someone you trust — a friend, family member, or the police. You are not the first person this has happened to and you will not be the last.
You are now digitally literate. You know how to type, organise files, write emails, and stay safe online. Those four skills are the foundation of everything else on a computer. Well done.
