You have the skills. You have the projects. Now you need a job. This course is about the last mile — everything between "I can build things" and "I have a paycheck."

What employers actually want

Most hiring managers are not looking for the best coder. They are looking for the lowest-risk hire. Someone who will:

  • Show up on time
  • Communicate clearly
  • Ask for help when stuck
  • Learn without being told
  • Not cause drama
  • Ship work that works

Technical skill gets you shortlisted. Everything else gets you hired.

The four signals that win interviews

  1. You have shipped real things — not just tutorials. Live URLs, working apps, code on GitHub.
  2. You can explain your code — why you made the choices you made, not just what the code does.
  3. You are easy to work with — clear communication, no ego, curious questions.
  4. You want to grow — you read, you learn, you ask what you do not know.

The reality of the search

Job searching is a numbers game. A realistic first-time search in Malawi:

  • Apply to 30–50 positions
  • Get 5–10 first-round replies
  • Get 2–3 interviews
  • Land 1 job offer

That is not failure — it is the base rate. Even strong candidates go through it. The people who get hired are the ones who keep applying after the tenth rejection.

The mindset: the search itself is a project. Treat it like one — with deadlines, metrics, and iteration. The person who sends 5 tailored applications per week beats the person who sends 1 perfect application per month.

Your portfolio is not a museum of your work. It is a sales pitch. A hiring manager spends 30 seconds deciding whether to keep reading. Your portfolio's job is to make them want more.

The 5-second test

Open your portfolio on a phone. Show it to someone for 5 seconds. Close it. Ask them:

  • What does this person do?
  • What have they built?
  • How would you hire them?

If they cannot answer all three, the portfolio needs work.

Choosing your 3–5 projects

Quality beats quantity. A portfolio with 3 strong projects beats one with 12 weak ones. Rules:

  • Live URL required — a project not on the internet is not a project
  • Real users preferred — even if it is your aunt's shop
  • One "flagship" — your best work, the one you are proudest of
  • Variety if possible — one web app, one API, one data project
  • Recent — projects from 3+ years ago say "I stopped learning"

Describing each project

For each project, answer four questions:

  1. What does it do? One sentence.
  2. What problem does it solve? One sentence.
  3. What did you build? Technology stack, key features.
  4. What was hard? One specific challenge you solved.

That last one is what separates a portfolio from a list. It shows you think.

The portfolio pitfalls

  • No live link — the fastest way to be ignored
  • Only screenshots — screenshots can be faked; live URLs cannot
  • Vague descriptions — "a website I made" tells me nothing
  • Too many projects — 20 mediocre projects hide your best work
  • Broken links — check every project URL monthly
  • Mobile-hostile — most recruiters check on their phone first
  • Slow to load — over 500 KB and you are losing readers

Recruiters look at your GitHub. Not always, but often enough that it matters. A clean, active GitHub profile is a real asset.

What recruiters check

  1. Do you have pinned repos? — the first thing they see
  2. Are your projects recent? — a profile with nothing in 2 years says "inactive"
  3. Do your READMEs explain anything? — or are they empty
  4. Are your commits meaningful? — or all "asdf"
  5. Is your profile README filled in? — name, what you do, how to reach you

The profile README

Create a repo with the exact same name as your username. GitHub shows its README at the top of your profile. It is your GitHub front page.

A good profile README is short:

# Chisomo Banda

Frontend developer based in Lilongwe, Malawi.

I build fast, mobile-first websites for small businesses.

## What I am working on
- [Portfolio site](https://chisomo.dev)
- React dashboard for a local logistics company

## Tech
HTML · CSS · JavaScript · React · Node.js · Python

## Reach me
- Email: chisomo@example.mw
- LinkedIn: [chisomo-banda](https://linkedin.com/in/chisomo-banda)
- WhatsApp: +265 999 555 123

That is it. Five sections. Thirty seconds to read. Every recruiter leaves with a clear picture.

Pinned repositories

Pin 4–6 repos. Not 20. The pinned repos should be:

  • Your best work, not your oldest
  • Complete, not half-finished experiments
  • Named clearly (see next section)
  • Each with a real README

Naming your repos

  • Bad: project1, test, my-app, final-version-v2
  • Good: weather-dashboard, todo-react-app, blog-api-node

The name should tell the reader what the project is. Same as file names — clarity beats cleverness.

Six sections. Anyone who reads it knows:

Commit messages

Recruiters do not read every commit. But they see the pattern. Meaningful messages signal professionalism.

  • Bad: fix, update, asdf, stuff, final
  • Good: Add search bar to header, Fix login redirect on session expiry

The contribution graph

Green squares look nice, but do not game them. One real commit a week beats 50 fake ones. If you are actively learning, the graph will show it naturally.

What to never commit

  • .env files with secrets
  • node_modules
  • API keys, passwords, tokens
  • Build output (dist/, build/)
  • Large binary files (videos, datasets)

In Malawi, LinkedIn is the single most important professional network. Recruiters search it before they post jobs. NGOs use it to source candidates. Remote-first companies use it to find talent in Africa.

The three things recruiters see

  1. Your photo — 6× more clicks if you have one
  2. Your headline — the line under your name
  3. Your About section — the first two lines, before "see more"

Everything else matters, but those three decide whether they read further.

Your photo

You do not need a professional photographer. You need:

  • A clear face, well lit, looking at the camera
  • Plain background (a wall works)
  • A plain shirt or one without logos
  • A smile — you are showing you are approachable

Take 20 photos on your phone against a window. Pick the best one. Done.

Your headline

The default headline is just your current job title. That wastes the most valuable line on your profile.

Strong headlines follow this pattern:

[What you do] | [Who you do it for] | [What makes you different]

Examples:

  • Frontend developer | Building fast, mobile-first websites for Malawian businesses
  • Data analyst | Turning programme data into decisions for NGOs
  • Mobile developer | Flutter apps for Android and iOS | Open to remote roles
  • Backend engineer | Python + PostgreSQL | Building APIs for African fintech

Your About section

Not a CV. A short, human paragraph. Structure:

  1. Who you are — one sentence
  2. What you do — one or two sentences
  3. What you have built — one or two examples with a link
  4. What you are looking for — one sentence
  5. How to reach you — email, portfolio link

Example:

I am a frontend developer based in Lilongwe. I build fast, mobile-first websites for small businesses and NGOs.

Over the last year I have built 6 production websites, including a booking system for a local tour company and a shop site for a coffee exporter. My portfolio is at chisomo.dev.

I am currently looking for junior frontend roles — local or remote. I am especially interested in teams that care about performance on slow connections.

Best way to reach me: chisomo@example.mw.

Under 120 words. Everything a recruiter needs.

Getting the most out of LinkedIn

  • Post once a week. What you learned, what you built, what confused you. Real posts beat curated perfection.
  • Connect with everyone you meet. Classmates, instructors, anyone in tech in Malawi.
  • Follow companies you want to work for. You will see their posts when they hire.
  • Comment on posts. A thoughtful comment on a hiring manager's post is more effective than 20 cold applications.
  • Use the "Open to Work" feature — recruiters filter by it.
  • Fill in skills. LinkedIn search matches on skills. List 20–30 relevant ones.

NGO and remote-work angle

If you are targeting NGOs or remote work:

  • Add English fluency in languages section
  • Mention timezone flexibility in About if applying for remote roles outside Africa
  • Highlight any international collaboration — even online study groups count
  • Add portfolio link directly in the headline if short enough

Your CV opens doors. Your cover letter or cold email walks you through them. Both need to be short, specific, and tailored.

The CV that gets read

Most CVs fail because they are too long, too vague, or too generic. A good junior CV in Malawi is:

  • 1 page — always, unless you have 10+ years of experience
  • Reverse chronological — most recent first
  • Simple — no graphics, no columns, no fancy templates
  • ATS-friendly — plain text, standard headings, no tables
  • Tailored — adjust the top third for each application

The interview is where you turn a maybe into a yes. Most junior candidates fail not because they lack skill, but because they cannot communicate it.

The three interview types

  1. Behavioural — "Tell me about a time when…"
  2. Technical — "How would you build…"
  3. Take-home or live coding — here is a problem, solve it

You will face a mix. Prepare for each.

Behavioural: the STAR method

Every behavioural question wants a story. Structure it as:

  • Situation — one sentence setting the scene
  • Task — what you had to do
  • Action — what you actually did
  • Result — what happened

Example — "Tell me about a time you solved a hard problem."

Situation: On a client project last year, the checkout page was loading in 8 seconds on mobile.

Task: I had two days to get it under 2 seconds or the client would lose customers.

Action: I compressed the hero image from 1.4 MB to 60 KB, removed two unused JavaScript libraries, and moved the remaining scripts to load after the page rendered.

Result: Load time dropped to 1.3 seconds and the client reported a 22% increase in completed orders that month.

Notice: specific numbers, clear actions, real outcome. That is what interviewers want.

Technical: the framework

When asked "how would you build X," you do not need the perfect answer. You need to show your thinking. Framework:

  1. Clarify the requirements. Ask questions: "Who uses this? How many users? Mobile only or desktop too?"
  2. State your assumptions. "I will assume under 1,000 users, mobile-first, no login required."
  3. Sketch the approach. Frontend, backend, database, deployment.
  4. Describe trade-offs. "I could use React for interactivity, or plain JavaScript for simplicity — I would choose plain JavaScript for this scale."
  5. Mention what you would learn. "I have not used Redis before, but I would research caching options."

Asking good questions is more impressive than having the perfect answer. Interviewers are looking for how you think, not what you have memorised.

Live coding

You will be nervous. Everyone is. Rules:

  • Think out loud. Say what you are doing and why. Silence is the enemy.
  • Start simple. Get something working, then improve it.
  • Test as you go. Use console.log or print statements.
  • Ask for help if stuck. "I am not sure how to do X — could you point me in the right direction?" This is a good sign, not a weakness.
  • Reflect afterwards. "If I had more time, I would refactor this part."

Questions you should ask them

At the end of the interview, they will ask if you have questions. Have three ready:

  • "What does a typical week look like for this role?"
  • "What would success look like in the first 90 days?"
  • "How does the team handle code reviews and learning?"

Good questions signal that you are serious about the role, not just looking for any job.

You have an offer. Now for the part most people get wrong — negotiation. It is not aggressive, awkward, or risky. It is expected, and it is worth real money.

Why you should always negotiate

Two reasons:

  1. The first offer is almost never the best offer. Employers build in room for the negotiation.
  2. The number compounds. Your starting salary shapes every future raise, bonus, and job offer.

A 15% increase at the start can mean millions of kwacha over a career.

What is actually negotiable

  • Base salary — the biggest lever
  • Signing bonus — often easier to get than a higher base
  • Start date — sometimes worth weeks of flexibility
  • Remote days — for hybrid roles
  • Training budget — courses, conferences, certifications
  • Equipment — a laptop, a phone, data allowance
  • Review timing — when your next salary review happens
  • Job title — matters for your next job search

You will not get all of these. Pick the two that matter most and push on those.

The script

When you receive an offer, do not say yes or no immediately. Say:

"Thank you — I am very excited about this role. I would like 24 hours to review the details. Is that okay?"

Almost always, yes. Now you have time to think. Then, when you are ready:

"Thank you again. I am excited to accept — but before I do, I would like to discuss the salary. Based on my research and the local market for this role, I was expecting something in the range of [number] – [number]. Is there any flexibility to get closer to that?"

Three things this script does:

  • It signals you want the job (you are already accepting)
  • It gives a range, not a demand
  • It asks a question instead of making a statement

How to choose your number

  1. Research. Ask peers, check Glassdoor, look at NGO salary scales.
  2. Set a target. What you want, ideally 10–20% above the offer.
  3. Set a walk-away. The minimum you would actually accept. Do not go below this.
  4. Set a range. Target at the top, walk-away at the bottom.

The three responses you will get

"Yes, we can do that."

Great. Accept politely, ask for the revised offer in writing. Do not gloat.

"We cannot go higher on base, but we can offer…"

Consider the alternative. If it is training budget, a review in 6 months, or a signing bonus, that can be worth as much as base salary. Ask what the value is and decide.

"That is our final offer."

If it is above your walk-away, take it. If it is below and they will not move, thank them and walk away politely. There is almost always another job.

What not to do

  • Do not name a number first — if you can, ask their range. If they insist, give your range, not a single number.
  • Do not bluff — do not say you have another offer if you do not. It can end the conversation.
  • Do not apologise — negotiating is normal. Do not say "sorry to ask."
  • Do not accept immediately — even if the offer is great, thanking them and asking for 24 hours is always correct.

For remote roles in USD

Remote salaries are often pegged to the country you live in. Some companies try to pay Malawian rates. Push back:

"I understand the cost-of-living adjustment. However, my work is the same regardless of my location, and I have delivered projects on the same stack as your team. I am looking for a rate between $X and $Y based on the value I bring, not just my geography."

This does not always work. But it works more often than most people think, especially for skilled technical roles.

The mindset: negotiating is not asking for a favour. It is a normal business conversation. The worst outcome is that they say no and you accept the original offer — which is where you would have been anyway.

The honest truth

Some of you will get a job in 4 weeks. Most will take 2–4 months. Some will take 6. It depends on the market, your target, and luck.

What is in your control:

  • The number of applications you send
  • The quality of your materials
  • How well you communicate
  • How you handle rejection
  • Whether you keep going

Everyone who gets hired has one thing in common: they did not stop.
