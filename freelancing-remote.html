Freelancing means selling your skills directly to clients — usually online, usually in USD or EUR — without an employer in the middle. For a skilled Malawian, this can mean earning 5 to 30 times what a local salary pays for the same work.

The numbers

A junior web developer in Lilongwe might earn MK 400,000 per month. That is roughly $230 USD. The same person, working 20 hours a week for a UK client at £15/hour, earns £1,200/month — about MK 2,000,000. Same skills. Same effort. Different client market.

This is not a fantasy. Thousands of developers in Kenya, Nigeria, India, and the Philippines earn exactly this way. Malawi is not yet saturated with freelancers — that is your advantage.

What freelancing is not

  • Not passive income. You work for every dollar. Some months are quiet. Some are busy.
  • Not easy money. Your first client will be hard to land. Your second will be easier. Your tenth will be almost automatic.
  • Not for everyone. If you hate writing, meeting deadlines, or dealing with clients, this is not the path for you. Local employment might suit you better — that is fine.

What freelancers actually do

The most common freelance services in the markets Malawians can access:

  • Web development — building sites, landing pages, WordPress work, small web apps
  • Mobile development — Flutter, React Native apps
  • Backend / API work — building or fixing Node, Python, or PHP services
  • Data work — cleaning datasets, building dashboards, small analyses
  • Design — UI/UX, logos, social media graphics
  • Writing — blog posts, technical documentation, product descriptions
  • Virtual assistance — email management, scheduling, customer support

You do not need to be the best in the world at any of these. You need to be reliable and communicative. Most clients would rather hire a competent, on-time freelancer than a brilliant one who ghosts them.

The mindset shift

As a freelancer, you are not an employee. You are a business.

  • You decide what to work on
  • You set your rates
  • You manage your time
  • You are responsible for getting paid

This is freedom, and it is also responsibility. Nobody will tell you to work today. Nobody will remind you that a deadline is approaching. That is on you.

The honest truth: freelancing is the most direct path from "I have skills" to "I earn money." It is also the path that requires the most discipline. If you can commit to 10 hours a week for six months, this course will show you exactly how to do it.

Choosing the right niche is the single biggest decision you make as a freelancer. "I do websites" gets lost in a sea of thousands. "I build fast landing pages for NGOs" gets hired.

What is a niche?

A niche is the intersection of three things:

  1. What you do — web development, data analysis, design
  2. Who you do it for — startups, churches, NGOs, e-commerce shops
  3. What specific problem you solve — fast-loading sites, mobile money integration, monthly reports

Example niches:

  • "I build low-bandwidth websites for Malawian small businesses"
  • "I set up payment integration for African e-commerce stores"
  • "I clean and analyse sales data for retail shops"
  • "I build Flutter apps for agri-tech startups"

Notice each one is specific. That specificity is what gets you hired.

Why narrow beats broad

Imagine you need a plumber. You have two choices:

  • Person A: "I do general handyman work"
  • Person B: "I specialise in bathroom plumbing"

If your bathroom is leaking, who do you call? Person B. Every time. The same applies online. Narrow niches:

  • Stand out in searches
  • Command higher rates because you are a specialist
  • Attract better clients who want exactly what you do
  • Build faster — you solve the same problem repeatedly, so you get faster at it

The fear of narrowing

"But if I narrow down, I will miss clients!" This is the most common fear, and it is wrong. Narrowing does not prevent you from taking other work. It just changes how you market yourself. You can still accept a general web project if it comes. But your profile says "landing page specialist," so that is what you get searched for.

How to pick your niche

Answer three questions:

  1. What can I do well right now? Be honest. Do not pick a niche you would need two years of training for.
  2. Who has this problem and money to pay? NGOs, e-commerce, small businesses, and international startups are common answers in Malawi's market.
  3. Can I describe the result in one sentence? If yes, you have a niche. If no, narrow further.

Your niche can change. Most freelancers refine theirs every six months as they learn what works. The goal is not to pick perfectly on day one — it is to pick something and start.

The classic mistake: "I do all kinds of work — coding, design, writing, data entry." That profile gets ignored because it signals "I have not decided what I am good at." Pick one. Add more later if you must.

On Upwork and Fiverr, your profile is your shopfront. Clients scroll through dozens of profiles. Yours needs to make them stop and click.

Upwork profile anatomy

Five parts, in order of importance:

  1. Title — one line, your niche
  2. Overview — a few paragraphs about what you do and who you help
  3. Skills — tags that Upwork uses for search
  4. Portfolio — 3 to 5 pieces of work
  5. Photo and location — a real photo of you, a professional one if possible

The title

Weak titles:

  • "Web Developer"
  • "Freelancer"
  • "Looking for work"

Strong titles:

  • "Landing Page Developer | Fast, Mobile-First, Loads in Under 2s"
  • "Flutter Mobile Developer | Android & iOS Apps"
  • "Data Analyst | Python, Pandas, Dashboards for African Businesses"

The strong titles do four things: they name the skill, the platform or result, the benefit, and a specific audience. Use the pattern:

[Skill] | [What you deliver] for [Who]

The overview

The overview is where you sell yourself. Not your history — your value. Structure:

  1. Hook — one sentence that names the client's pain and how you solve it
  2. What you do — one paragraph in plain language
  3. What you have built — 2 or 3 short bullet points or one example project
  4. What working with you is like — communication style, timezone, reliability
  5. Call to action — what the client should do next

Weak overviews start with "I am a passionate web developer with 3 years of experience." Strong overviews start with the client: "Your landing page has 3 seconds to convince a visitor. I build pages that load in under 2 seconds even on 2G — because I learned on 2G."

The portfolio

Three to five pieces. For each one, include:

  • A screenshot or live link
  • One sentence on the problem
  • One sentence on what you did
  • A result if you have one

If you have no paid work yet, use personal projects. Every course project you built in HTML/CSS, JavaScript, React, Python, Node, Flutter, or Data Analysis is a portfolio piece. Put them on GitHub Pages or Netlify and link them.

Fiverr — the other model

Fiverr works differently. Instead of bidding on jobs, you list gigs — predefined services at fixed prices. A gig has:

  • Title — same rules as Upwork titles
  • Description
  • Packages — Basic, Standard, Premium at different prices and deliverables
  • Gallery — up to 3 images or a video

Fiverr is faster to start but lower-priced. Many Malawian freelancers use Fiverr for volume and Upwork for higher-paying clients.

The biggest profile mistake: copying someone else's. Clients can spot it instantly. Write yours in your own voice — even imperfect English reads better than a template.

Your Upwork or Fiverr profile matters for getting found. Your portfolio site matters for closing the deal. When a client is deciding between two freelancers, they check the portfolio.

Why you need your own site

  • Control. Upwork can suspend your account tomorrow. Your own domain is yours forever.
  • Professionalism. chisomo.dev looks better than a Upwork profile URL.
  • Proof of skill. A clean, fast portfolio site is itself a portfolio piece — especially for developers.
  • SEO. When someone searches "Malawian Flutter developer," your site can be the top result.

What to include

Five sections, no more:

  1. Hero — your name, what you do, one line on who you help
  2. Selected work — 3 to 5 projects, each with a screenshot, a link, and 2–3 sentences
  3. About — a short paragraph on who you are and how you work
  4. Contact — email, WhatsApp, LinkedIn
  5. Footer — copyright, links

You do not need a blog, a services page, pricing, or a "book a call" widget. Keep it simple. Clients are busy.

The tech stack

Plain HTML and CSS. That is it. You can build this in one evening with the skills from the HTML & CSS course. If you want a bit of interactivity, add a small amount of JavaScript.

Do not use React, Next.js, or a framework for your portfolio. Reason: your portfolio is a landing page. It should load in under 2 seconds on a slow phone. Frameworks make that harder, not easier.

Hosting

Three free options that just work:

  • GitHub Pages — push to a repo called yourname.github.io
  • Netlify — drag and drop your folder
  • Vercel — same idea, good for React projects

A custom domain costs $10–15/year. Optional but worth it once you have your first client.

The GitHub profile

Many clients, especially technical ones, will look at your GitHub. Make sure:

  • Your profile README introduces you — name, niche, contact
  • Your pinned repos show your 4–6 best projects, not your random learning experiments
  • Each project has a README with what it does, how to run it, and a screenshot
  • Commit messages are meaningful — no "asdf" or "final final v2"

The overall checklist

  • Domain name (optional but recommended)
  • Portfolio site with 3+ projects
  • GitHub profile with pinned repos and a README
  • Upwork profile complete (all sections)
  • Fiverr gigs, if you want volume
  • LinkedIn profile with a professional photo and updated headline

This whole setup takes one weekend. It is the single highest-leverage weekend you will spend as a freelancer — everything else builds on top of it.

The 5-second test: open your portfolio on your phone. If a stranger cannot tell within 5 seconds what you do and how to hire you, redesign it. That is the entire goal of the site.

Profiles get you found. Proposals get you hired. This is where the money is made or lost.

The reality

On Upwork, most freelancers send 20–50 proposals before landing their first job. That is normal. Do not be discouraged. Every rejection is information — read the client's response (or silence) as feedback.

The anatomy of a winning proposal

A proposal has five parts, and it is short. Most successful proposals are 100–200 words. Long proposals get skimmed and discarded.

  1. Hook — one sentence proving you read the job post
  2. Understanding — one sentence showing you know the problem
  3. Solution — one or two sentences on how you will solve it
  4. Proof — one link to a relevant past project
  5. Call to action — one sentence asking for the next step

Weak vs strong proposal

Weak (the one everyone sends):

Dear Sir/Madam,

I am a passionate web developer with 5 years of experience.
I have worked on many projects and I am confident I can do
this job. Please consider my application.

Thank you,
Chisomo

Strong (specific, short, focused):

Hi Sarah,

I noticed your site loads slowly on mobile — I checked it
on my phone and it took 8 seconds. That is likely costing
you customers.

I build fast, low-bandwidth landing pages. Here is a
recent one I did for a Malawian startup that loads in
1.4 seconds on 3G: [link]

If you would like, I can do a free 10-minute audit and
send you three specific fixes. No obligation.

Chisomo Banda

Notice the differences:

  • The strong one mentions a specific detail from the job
  • It names the problem in the client's language
  • It shows a real example
  • It offers a low-risk next step
  • It is half the length

Personalisation is not optional

Upwork shows clients how many proposals you have sent that week. It also flags copy-paste proposals. If your proposal could be sent to any job in the category, rewrite it. Real personalisation takes 3 minutes per proposal. That is 3 minutes that could earn you $300.

The three proposal killers

  1. Starting with yourself. "I am a developer..." — the client does not care. Start with their problem.
  2. Listing everything you can do. Sounding desperate. Pick the one thing that matches this job.
  3. Making the client work. "Please see my profile for details." Do not send them hunting. Give the answer right there.

A volume strategy that works

  • Send 3–5 quality proposals per day, personalised
  • Track them in a simple spreadsheet — job title, client, date sent, follow-up date
  • Do not check email every 5 minutes. Check twice a day.
  • Expect 5–10% to reply, 2–3% to hire you

That math means: 100 proposals → 5–10 replies → 2–3 hires. Two or three clients from 100 proposals. If you send 5 proposals a day, five days a week, that is 100 in a month. You could realistically land your first two clients in month one.

The one rule: every proposal should take 3 to 5 minutes to write. If you spend 30 minutes on one, you are overthinking. If you spend 10 seconds on one, you are wasting everyone's time.

Upwork and Fiverr are where most freelancers start. But the biggest long-term gains come from direct outreach — emailing or messaging potential clients directly.

Why direct outreach works

  • No competition. On Upwork, you are one of 50 proposals. In a client's inbox, you are one of one.
  • Higher rates. Clients who come to you are less price-sensitive.
  • Longer relationships. A cold email that converts often becomes a multi-year client.
  • No platform fees. Upwork takes 10–20%. Direct clients take zero.

Who to email

Start with businesses you can actually help:

  • Local Malawi businesses with a website problem — check their site. Is it slow? Does it work on mobile? Does it have broken links? These are your reasons to email them.
  • NGOs that need help with data, websites, or digital tools
  • Startups on Twitter or LinkedIn announcing that they are hiring or launching
  • E-commerce shops that sell on Facebook or WhatsApp and might want a proper site
  • Church or community organisations that need help with digital presence

The cold email structure

Short. Specific. No fluff.

Subject: Quick observation about [specific thing on their site]

Hi [Name],

I was looking at [their site/business] and noticed
[specific problem] — the contact form on mobile is
broken, for example.

I build [your niche]. Here is a recent project: [link].

If you would like, I can send you a free 5-minute video
showing exactly what I would fix. No obligation.

[Your name]
[Phone or WhatsApp]

Four sentences. Notice:

  • Subject line is specific — not "Business proposal"
  • Opening proves you looked at their actual business
  • You name one specific problem, not five
  • You offer something free and specific (a 5-minute video, an audit)
  • No pressure, no pitch

The follow-up

70% of replies come from the second or third email, not the first. If you do not hear back in 5 days:

Subject: Re: Quick observation about [their site]

Hi [Name],

Just following up — I know inboxes get busy.

If the timing is not right, no worries at all.
I will check back in a few weeks.

[Your name]

Then send one more a week later. After three attempts, move on. Some clients will reach out months later when they are ready.

Where to find work

Beyond Upwork and Fiverr:

  • Remote job boards — Remote OK, We Work Remotely, Remote.co, Working Nomads
  • Twitter/X — follow "hiring" accounts and tech founders
  • LinkedIn — search "looking for [skill] freelancer" every morning
  • Facebook Groups — "Malawi Tech", "African Freelancers", "NGO Digital Support"
  • Word of mouth — tell every person you know what you do
  • Contra, Toptal, Braintrust — vetted platforms, higher pay, harder to get in

Handling rejection

You will get far more no than yes. That is not failure — it is normal. Professional freelancers have hundreds of rejections in their history. What matters is the ratio over time.

  • Track what works: which subject lines get opens, which openings get replies
  • Iterate every week
  • Never take it personally
The confidence trick: the first proposal feels terrifying. The tenth feels normal. The hundredth feels like brushing your teeth. Volume solves almost every psychological barrier in freelancing.

You have a client. You have done the work. Now you have to actually get the money. This is where many Malawian freelancers hit their biggest obstacle — international payments.

The challenge

PayPal does not allow withdrawals to Malawian bank accounts. Stripe does not support Malawi as a payout country. Direct bank transfers from the US or EU take 5–10 business days and cost $25–45 per transfer.

Fortunately, there are three reliable paths that work well from Malawi.

Payoneer

What it is: a US-based financial service that gives you a virtual US bank account. Clients can pay you like they would pay any US company.

Fees:

  • Free to set up
  • Receiving money from another Payoneer user: free
  • Receiving via US ACH or wire: 1%
  • Withdrawing to a Malawian bank: $1.50 per transaction, takes 1–2 business days
  • Currency conversion markup: about 2%

Verdict: the most popular choice for Malawian freelancers. Reliable, low fees, wide acceptance.

Wise

What it is: a UK-based service with real exchange rates. You get multi-currency account details in USD, EUR, GBP.

Fees:

  • Free to receive in most currencies
  • Conversion fee: 0.4–0.9% — much lower than banks
  • Withdrawal to Malawian bank: depends on the bank, usually a small flat fee

Verdict: the best exchange rates. Slightly more complex to set up. Worth it once you are earning $500+/month.

Other options

  • Skrill — similar to Payoneer, works in Malawi, higher fees
  • Direct bank wire — works but expensive ($25–45 per transfer). Only for large payments.
  • Crypto (USDT, USDC) — cheap and fast, but volatile and legally grey in some contexts. Use only if you understand the risk.
  • Upwork itself — if you use Upwork, they handle payments through their own system and you can withdraw to Payoneer or bank

Which to choose

If you are just starting:

  1. Set up Payoneer first. It is the easiest and most widely accepted.
  2. Add Wise once you have a regular client and want to save on exchange rates.
  3. Use Upwork's built-in payment if you are working through Upwork.

Set up both before you land your first client. The last thing you want is a client ready to pay you and no way to receive it.

Getting paid by a client directly

For direct clients, the standard process:

  1. Send a professional invoice (PDF or a tool like Wave, Zoho Invoice, or a simple template)
  2. Include payment instructions: "Pay via Payoneer to [email]" or "Pay via Wise to [account details]"
  3. Give the client 14 or 30 days to pay — standard terms
  4. Follow up if payment is late

Half up front, half on delivery is standard for new clients. Once trust is established, you can invoice the full amount on completion.

The hard truth: you will sometimes not get paid. Chasing a late client is part of the job. Set clear terms, invoice promptly, follow up politely. Most clients pay. A few will not. Build a buffer so one bad client does not sink you.

Every freelancer has to answer the question: how much do I charge? Get this wrong and you either starve or scare off every client. Get it right and you build a sustainable business.

How not to price

  • "What are other Malawians charging?" — the wrong question. You are not competing with Malawians. You are competing with the global market.
  • "What is the minimum I need to survive?" — this anchors you to poverty rates forever.
  • "What feels fair?" — feelings are a bad guide to business.

The right way to price

Start with your target income, work backwards.

  1. Decide what you want to earn per month in USD (start realistic — $200/month is fine for month one)
  2. Decide how many billable hours per week you can work (10 is realistic as a side hustle, 20–30 if full-time)
  3. Multiply: hourly rate = monthly target ÷ (hours per week × 4.33 weeks)

Example: target $400/month, 10 billable hours per week. Rate = $400 ÷ (10 × 4.33) = $9.25/hour.

That feels low. It is. Starting rates always are. The point is that as you build reviews and references, you raise the rate.

Rate progression

Typical progression for a Malawian freelancer:

  • Month 1–3: $5–10/hour — you are building reviews
  • Month 4–6: $10–20/hour — you have 5+ reviews, repeat clients
  • Month 7–12: $20–35/hour — you have a niche and a portfolio
  • Year 2: $35–60/hour — you are specialised and in demand
  • Year 3+: $60–120/hour — top of your niche, picky about clients

Notice the multiplier. The same skills, at a higher rate, because of reputation. Your job as a freelancer is to constantly build reputation.

Fixed price vs hourly

Both have their place:

  • Hourly — good for open-ended work, ongoing maintenance, or clients who change requirements constantly. Protects you from scope creep.
  • Fixed price — good for well-defined projects. The client knows the cost up front, which is reassuring. But you must estimate carefully — if you underestimate, you eat the loss.

A rule: if you are new to the client or the project is unclear, use hourly. If the client is trustworthy and the scope is tight, use fixed price.

Raising your rate

Every 3 months, ask yourself: "Am I turning away work because I am too busy?" If yes, raise your rate. Every 6 months, raise it 10–20% regardless. Existing clients can be grandfathered at their old rate for a few months, then transitioned.

Raising rates is scary. But the reality is: 90% of clients will accept the new rate without a word. 10% will negotiate or leave. You lose a little, you gain a lot.

Invoicing

Every invoice should include:

  • Your name and business name (if you have one)
  • Your contact details and payment details
  • The client's name and address
  • An invoice number (start at 001 and count up)
  • The date issued and the date due (14 or 30 days later)
  • A line for each deliverable, quantity, rate, amount
  • Total, currency, taxes (if any)
  • Payment terms — how and when to pay

Free tools that handle this: Wave, Zoho Invoice, or a simple template in Google Docs. Do not overthink it.

Tracking your income

Keep a simple spreadsheet:

  • Date | Client | Project | Amount USD | Payment method | Received?

Update it every time you invoice. At the end of the month, total the "Received" column. This is your actual income. It is also your record for tax purposes and your motivation on bad weeks.

The mindset: you are not "just a freelancer." You are a business owner. You set the rates. You choose the clients. You send the invoices. The moment you internalise that, everything else follows.

The hardest part of freelancing is not the work. It is the clients. Learning to communicate clearly, set boundaries, and handle difficult situations is what separates freelancers who burn out from those who build a career.

The first message

When a client agrees to work with you, the first exchange sets the tone. Send a short kickoff message confirming:

  • What you understood the project to be
  • What you will deliver, specifically
  • What you need from them (access, files, decisions)
  • Timeline — when they get what
  • Payment terms — how much, when

Example:

Hi Sarah,

Great to be working with you. Here is what I understood:

Deliverable: A new landing page with 4 sections and a
contact form. Mobile-friendly, loads in under 2 seconds.

What I need from you:
- Logo file
- Text content for all 4 sections (or I can draft it)
- Brand colors (if you have them)

Timeline:
- Draft by Friday 15th
- Revisions by Wednesday 20th
- Final delivery by Friday 22nd

Payment: $400 total, $200 deposit to start, $200 on delivery.

Please confirm everything looks right and send the files
when you can. I'll start immediately.

Thanks!
Chisomo

This message prevents 90% of the arguments freelancers have with clients. It creates a shared written record of what was agreed.

Communication frequency

Silence makes clients anxious. Aim for:

  • Weekly update — even if nothing has changed, "Still on track, delivery Friday" is fine
  • Immediate response to problems — if something breaks or a timeline slips, tell them before they notice
  • Respond within 24 hours on weekdays. Same day is better.

Timezone discipline

If your client is in London (UTC+0), and you are in Lilongwe (UTC+2), you have a 2-hour window where you are both working. Use it. Schedule calls, decisions, and important messages for that window. Save solo work for your own evening.

For US clients (UTC−5 to UTC−8), the gap is 7–10 hours. You may need to work evenings or early mornings occasionally for calls. Discuss this up front — most clients are understanding if you tell them early.

Managing expectations

Under-promise. Over-deliver. This is not manipulative — it is professional. If a project will take 5 days, tell the client 7. Then deliver on day 5. They are thrilled. If you promise 5 and deliver on 7, they are annoyed. Same result, different feeling.

When things go wrong

  1. Tell them immediately. Do not hide a problem hoping it will fix itself.
  2. Come with a solution. "I hit a delay because of X. Here is my plan to fix it." Not just "Sorry, I am behind."
  3. Own it. Even if it is partially their fault, take responsibility for your part. This builds enormous trust.
  4. Fix it fast. Ship a partial solution, a fix, an alternative. Action over apology.
The reputation rule: a client who sees you handle a problem well trusts you more than a client who never sees a problem at all. Crisis is an opportunity to prove you are reliable.

Now the harder stuff. Every freelancer eventually meets a client who is difficult, vague, slow to pay, or wants more than they paid for. Here is how to handle it.

Scope creep

Scope creep is when a client slowly asks for more and more, without paying more. "Can you just add this one small thing?" Thirty small things later, you have worked three times the agreed amount for the same fee.

How to handle it, gently:

That is a great idea. It is outside the original scope,
so I can add it for $X, or we can keep it for a follow-up
phase. Which would you prefer?

You are not refusing. You are pricing. Good clients accept this instantly. Bad clients push back — and that is useful information.

The client who goes silent

Two days after you send a question, no response. A week later, still nothing. What do you do?

  • Day 3: Friendly follow-up. "Just checking in on my question about X."
  • Day 7: Second follow-up with a clear decision point. "If I do not hear back by Friday, I will proceed with the original plan and adjust later."
  • Day 14: Escalate. "I want to make sure everything is OK. Is this project still a priority?"

If the silence continues and you have not been paid, stop work. Do not keep delivering to a client who is not engaging. Save your energy for clients who respect your time.

The client who is never happy

Some clients will always find something to criticise. Every deliverable has a problem. Nothing is ever quite right. This is a red flag — but there is a way to handle it:

  1. Set explicit revision rounds. "This package includes two rounds of revisions. Additional rounds are $X per hour."
  2. Ask for specific feedback. "What specifically would you like changed?" Vague dissatisfaction cannot be fixed. Concrete requests can.
  3. Document everything. If the relationship sours, you want a paper trail.
  4. Be willing to walk away. If the client is abusive, do not keep working. Refund what is fair, close the project, and move on.

Late payment

Standard escalation:

  • Day 1 past due: Polite reminder. "Just following up on invoice #014."
  • Day 7 past due: Firmer. "Please let me know when I can expect payment."
  • Day 14 past due: Direct. "I cannot continue work on the next phase until the outstanding invoice is settled."
  • Day 30 past due: Stop work. Send a final notice. Consider whether to continue the relationship.

Prevention is the best cure: 50% deposit up front, milestones for long projects, and clear payment terms on every invoice.

Red flags to watch for

End the conversation before you take on the client if you see:

  • Refusal to pay a deposit. Every legitimate client pays a deposit for a new freelancer.
  • Extreme urgency. "I need this tomorrow." Big projects take time. Urgency is often a red flag for a badly organised client.
  • Vague requirements. "Just build me something modern." You will never satisfy this client.
  • Poor communication from day one. It only gets worse.
  • Refusal to sign or confirm anything in writing. Every professional client is fine with a written scope. The ones who refuse have a reason.

When to fire a client

You are allowed to end a client relationship. Do it when:

  • You dread opening their messages
  • They consistently pay late
  • They cross professional boundaries (insults, inappropriate messages)
  • They are taking up 80% of your time for 20% of your income

End it professionally: "I have enjoyed working with you, but I am no longer able to continue on this project. I will finish the current deliverable and hand over any remaining work. Best of luck with everything."

You do not owe an explanation. You do not owe an apology. You owe a clean handover.

You have finished the course. You now know how freelancing works from start to finish: picking a niche, building a profile, finding clients, getting paid, and handling every kind of client behaviour. The rest is practice. The first client is the hardest. Everything after that is a repetition of what you just learned.
